Try this. Lower i₀ to 10⁻⁶ A cm⁻²: the flat region around η = 0 widens and the electrode becomes strongly polarizable, because hundreds of millivolts are needed before any appreciable current flows. Raise i₀ to 10⁻¹ A cm⁻² and the curve becomes almost vertical, the behaviour of a non-polarizable, reference-like electrode. Move α away from 0.5 and the curve loses its symmetry about η = 0. Heat the cell and notice that, at fixed i₀, the curve actually gets less steep, since the exponents scale with 1/T.

§1Where the equation comes from

Take a simple electrode reaction, O + ne⁻ ⇌ R, and assume the solution is well mixed so that surface concentrations equal bulk concentrations. At the equilibrium potential both directions still run, at equal and opposite rates; the magnitude of either partial current is the exchange current density, i₀.

Applying an overpotential η changes the energy of electrons in the metal and so shifts the free energy of O + ne⁻ relative to R by −nFη. Only a fraction of that shift reaches the transition state. The cathodic barrier rises by αnFη and the anodic barrier falls by (1 − α)nFη:

ΔGa‡=ΔG0‡−(1−α) nFηΔGc‡=ΔG0‡+α nFη\Delta G^{\ddagger}_{a} = \Delta G^{\ddagger}_{0} - (1-\alpha)\,nF\eta \qquad\qquad \Delta G^{\ddagger}_{c} = \Delta G^{\ddagger}_{0} + \alpha\,nF\eta

Each partial current is proportional to exp(−ΔG‡/RT), and both equal i₀ in magnitude at η = 0, so

ia=i0 exp⁡ ⁣((1−α) nFηRT)ic=− i0 exp⁡ ⁣(−α nFηRT)i_a = i_0\,\exp\!\left(\frac{(1-\alpha)\,nF\eta}{RT}\right) \qquad\qquad i_c = -\,i_0\,\exp\!\left(-\frac{\alpha\,nF\eta}{RT}\right)

Their sum is the Butler–Volmer equation. Move the sliders below and follow the two barriers.

Two remarks are worth stressing with graduate students. First, i₀ is not a material constant. For a single-step reaction it depends on the standard rate constant k⁰ and on the bulk concentrations:

i0=nFk0 (CO∗)1−α (CR∗)αi_0 = nFk^{0}\,(C_O^{*})^{1-\alpha}\,(C_R^{*})^{\alpha}

Second, writing the exponents as αn and (1 − α)n strictly applies to one elementary step. Real reactions such as hydrogen or oxygen evolution proceed through several steps, and the measured coefficients are apparent transfer coefficients, αa and αc, which need not add up to n. The 2014 IUPAC technical report on the transfer coefficient recommends this operational definition.

§2Two limits worth knowing by heart

Close to equilibrium. When nF|η|/RT ≪ 1, both exponentials can be expanded to first order and the current becomes proportional to the overpotential. The slope defines the charge-transfer resistance, the same quantity you fit as Rct in impedance spectra:

i≈i0 nFRT η⟹Rct=ηi=RTnF i0i \approx i_0\,\frac{nF}{RT}\,\eta \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad R_{ct} = \frac{\eta}{i} = \frac{RT}{nF\,i_0}

Far from equilibrium. When |η| is large, the reverse exponential becomes negligible and the logarithm of the current is linear in η. This is the Tafel equation, with slopes that carry the transfer coefficient:

η=± b log⁡10∣i∣i0,ba=2.303 RT(1−α) nF,bc=2.303 RTα nF\eta = \pm\, b\,\log_{10}\frac{|i|}{i_0}, \qquad b_a = \frac{2.303\,RT}{(1-\alpha)\,nF}, \qquad b_c = \frac{2.303\,RT}{\alpha\,nF}

The ratio of the reverse to the forward partial current is exp(−nF|η|/RT), independent of α, so the back reaction drops below 1% once |η| exceeds about 118/n mV at 25 °C. The linear approximation, by contrast, is within 1% only up to about 12 mV when α = 0.5, and only a few millivolts when α departs from 0.5.

§3When mass transport joins in

Everything so far assumed that surface concentrations equal bulk concentrations. At higher currents the reactant is consumed at the surface faster than it arrives, and the complete form of the equation keeps the surface-to-bulk ratios:

i=i0[CR(0)CR∗ e(1−α)nfη−CO(0)CO∗ e−αnfη],f=FRTi = i_0\left[\frac{C_R(0)}{C_R^{*}}\,e^{(1-\alpha)nf\eta} - \frac{C_O(0)}{C_O^{*}}\,e^{-\alpha nf\eta}\right], \qquad f=\frac{F}{RT}

With a steady diffusion layer, each ratio is fixed by the limiting current of its species:

CR(0)CR∗=1−iiL,a,CO(0)CO∗=1+i∣iL,c∣\frac{C_R(0)}{C_R^{*}} = 1-\frac{i}{i_{L,a}}, \qquad \frac{C_O(0)}{C_O^{*}} = 1+\frac{i}{|i_{L,c}|}

Substituting and solving for i gives a closed form that moves smoothly from kinetic control to transport control:

i=e(1−α)nfη−e−αnfη1i0+e(1−α)nfηiL,a+e−αnfη∣iL,c∣i = \frac{e^{(1-\alpha)nf\eta} - e^{-\alpha nf\eta}}{\dfrac{1}{i_0} + \dfrac{e^{(1-\alpha)nf\eta}}{i_{L,a}} + \dfrac{e^{-\alpha nf\eta}}{|i_{L,c}|}}

Far along one branch this reduces to the Koutecký–Levich form used with rotating disk electrodes to extract the purely kinetic current ik:

§4Worked examples

Example A. Why platinum and mercury behave so differently for hydrogen evolution

In the Tafel region the overpotential needed to reach a target current density follows directly from i₀ and the Tafel slope. The usual benchmark for water-splitting electrocatalysts is 10 mA cm⁻²:

Tafel slope

Every decade of i₀ costs one Tafel slope of overpotential. That is why catalyst development, including work on transition-metal phosphides such as FeP, targets both a high i₀ and a low Tafel slope.

Caution for discussion: the 40 and 30 mV dec⁻¹ values assume low hydrogen coverage and a symmetric barrier. A measured slope is a clue to the mechanism, not a proof of it.

      §5Check your understanding

      Two catalysts have the same Tafel slope, 120 mV dec⁻¹, but their exchange current densities differ by four orders of magnitude. How much extra overpotential does the poorer one need for the same current in the Tafel region?

      About 480 mV. The Tafel lines are parallel and shifted by four decades along log |i|, and each decade costs one Tafel slope.

      Why is an electrode with a very large i₀ a good reference electrode?

      Its charge-transfer resistance, RT/nFi₀, is tiny. The small currents drawn by the measuring circuit barely shift its potential, so it is effectively non-polarizable.

      A cathodic Tafel slope of 60 mV dec⁻¹ is measured at 25 °C for a reaction that consumes two electrons overall. What does it tell you?

      The apparent cathodic transfer coefficient is about 1 (59 mV/b). A single elementary one-electron step with α ≈ 0.5 would give about 118 mV dec⁻¹, so the data point to a multistep mechanism, typically a fast one-electron pre-equilibrium followed by a rate-determining chemical step. Coverage effects can produce similar slopes, so confirm with reaction orders or impedance.

      The cell is heated from 25 °C to 60 °C and α stays at 0.5. What happens to the Tafel slope and to i₀?

      The slope is proportional to T: it rises from 118 to 132 mV dec⁻¹. The exchange current density grows exponentially following an Arrhenius law with the apparent activation energy. In practice the larger i₀ usually wins, so the overpotential at a given current falls.

      If a Tafel line is fitted where mass transport already matters, are α and i₀ over- or underestimated?

      The points fall below the kinetic line as they approach the plateau, so the fitted slope is too large and α is underestimated. The flatter line usually extrapolates to a higher value at η = 0, overestimating i₀. Drag the window of Example C into the bend to see it.
